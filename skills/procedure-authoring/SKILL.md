# Writing, editing and checking procedures

## What this is

This is a meta-instruction, not a standard and not a workflow. It gives patterns: how to look at a procedure so that the text helps its reader. The method helps and teaches: the questions below suggest what to check, while the decision stays with the agent. It does not set a project's rules and policy — they have their own work. The agent decides whether to apply the questions to a whole text or to one phrase. A good procedure saves its reader from guessing: they do what they would otherwise do at random. The questions below are worth asking.

## What to ask

Questions are not mandatory fields: a text includes what serves its reader. The list is not exhaustive; "everything is mandatory" — no. The order is optional: take the questions the work needs.

- **Reader, moment, decision.** Who reads, at what moment, and what decision do they make? If there are no decisions, it is a description, not a procedure. A decision is what the reader will do differently.
- **Case.** Where will the reader err without the text? Turn "make it clearer" into such a case. Write it before the first line: the case shows whether the text helped.
- **Answer in the text.** Is the answer here, or does the reader go to another document? A walk for an answer loses the reader.
- **Minimum.** What does the reader do, what if a step fails, and what do they hand over? A step says what it rests on and how it ends. Without a failure path the reader drops the step; without a deliverable, it is unclear what counts as done.
- **Support.** What does the step rest on — data, access, a tool? Does the reader have it? Without support a step is invention.
- **Price.** What does a practice cost the reader, and what failure does it prevent? A practice without a price looks like a habit.
- **Measure.** Does every phrase pay off: does it add a decision, data or a description? A phrase without which the next reader would get it wrong is needed; the rest can go. Text is paid for by reading.
- **Header.** What is this, when does it apply, in what environment? From the header the reader decides whether to read on. The description speaks of the subject, not of the reader.
- **Form.** Do the form and order of sections follow the work? A template that does not fit the work gets in the way; a full list of defects is not needed.

## The check

Checking is needed so that the text does not promise more than it does.

- **Promise.** What did the text promise, and what does it do?
- **Header.** Does the header say the same as the body?
- **Step.** Does the step rest on what the reader has?
- **Sentence.** What does it add — a decision, data, a description?
- **Copies.** Does it repeat the text next to it?

Finding: an address, a quote, why this changes the decision, a fix, severity. Blocker — without the fix the reader errs in any case. Should — in a common case. Could — in a rare case or an improvement. What confirms it: a quote, a call with arguments, a number with a command; "it works" is not proof. Someone else's text is checked without edits: the deliverable is findings.
